## 0. Summary

- **Build first:** a cast-iron-style civic dome (Capitol type), a Gothic Revival clock-tower town hall (Manchester type), and a long colonnaded neoclassical block (Federal Triangle type). These give the most silhouette at speed on a 1.1 km straight.
- **Best domes:** (1) Capitol-type cast-iron dome on a drum with a ring of columns; (2) Dresden Frauenkirche-type stone bell dome, the most distinctive silhouette in the set; (3) Panthéon / St Paul's type stone dome on a drum, the cleanest mass for a hairpin landmark.
- **Third Reich grammar:** not recommended as a reference. Its signature buildings are recognisable Nazi landmarks, and the game rules and German law (section 5) make the risk real. The massing lesson can be learnt from Federal Triangle, which uses the same stripped-stone colonnade logic without the history.
- **Venetian clash:** none if the lower loop uses Gothic Revival and neoclassical civic buildings, which sit near Venetian Gothic (Ruskin's link). Domes are the one place to check against the upper half (section 4).
- **Could not verify:** a Poly Haven limestone set, a copper or verdigris set, a half-timber set, and any dome diameter for Frauenkirche, Reichstag or Altes Museum.

## 2. US Capitol style: domes and their European models

### 2.1 How the parts fit

- **Capitol (Washington):** Charles Bulfinch's 1818 low dome was replaced by a Thomas U. Walter cast-iron dome, built 1856–66 [F, aoc.gov and Wikipedia]. The dome is 100 ft (30 m) in diameter [F]. It stands on a ring of **36 Corinthian columns** around its base [F], with the Statue of Freedom on the tholos [F]. **Drum, lantern and dome height are not given in the sources I read** (see section 7).
- **Pattern (est.):** a stone or iron dome on a cylindrical drum, the drum wrapped by a colonnade (peristyle), then a lantern or tholos carrying the cap. Each element is a separate silhouette step: drum band, column ring, dome curve, lantern.
- **St Paul's (London):** Wren's three-part design: a lead-covered outer dome, a brick cone, and an inner dome, with a stone lantern at the top. The article says the inner dome and cone are each 18 inches thick [F]. Height **365 ft (111 m)**, which the article gives as the cathedral's height rather than to the cross [F].
- **Panthéon (Paris):** Soufflot's 1755 design. Corinthian peristyle with a triangular pediment on the east front [F]. Dome **83.0 m (272 ft)** [F]. The article describes it as "built entirely of stone" in three nested domes [F].
- **Les Invalides (Paris):** Hardouin-Mansart. Dome **107 m (351 ft)** in total, with a 90 m dome section topped by a lantern [F]. Stone, gilded. The dome is two domes stacked, and the lower one is open at its base [F].
- **St Peter's (Rome):** dome top at **136.6 m (448 ft)** [F]. The article does not say whether this is to the cross or the lantern.

### 2.2 Recommended dome references (from this family)

## 3. Third Reich architecture: history, not a reference

### 3.1 What the buildings were and who built them

- The regime used monumental architecture to "deliver and enforce their ideology." Forced labour, including SS-run camps such as Flossenbürg, quarried stone and made brick for Speer's projects. Many workers died [F, Wikipedia "Nazi architecture"].
- Speer ran building construction from 1934. Most of his plans for "Welthauptstadt Germania" were never built. Only a small part went up between 1937 and 1943 [F].
- The buildings below were built for the regime's events, offices and military and state use. Several were severely damaged or demolished after 1945 [F].

### 3.2 Buildings, with style notes

| Building | Date and builder | What is recorded | Game risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tempelhof Airport, Berlin | 1936–41 (never finished), Ernst Sagebiel [F] | 1.2 km curved terminal; "shell limestone" facades; the plan was "designed to resemble an eagle in flight" [F] | **High.** The eagle-shaped plan is itself a symbol. Do not copy the shape |
| Haus der Kunst, Munich | 1933–37, Paul Troost [F] | "Totalitarian classicism", built in stone; a swastika-motif mosaic in the portico ceiling [F] | **High.** Recognisable building; the mosaic is an insignia |
| Zeppelin Field grandstand, Nuremberg | Speer [F] | About 360 m wide; square piers, inspired by Paul Philippe Cret [F]; a large emblem on top was removed in 1945 [F]; double pillars blown up in 1967 [F] | **High.** Recognisable landmark, with emblem history |
| Berlin Olympiastadion | 1934–36, Werner March [F] | About 110,000 capacity in 1936; the lower half sunk 12 m below ground; a 77 m bell tower [F] | **Medium-high.** Identifiable 1936 Olympic landmark |
| Reich Chancellery, Berlin | 1938–39, Albert Speer [F] | Marble Gallery 147.5 m long; "stern, authoritarian" appearance; severely damaged in 1945 and demolished on Soviet orders [F] | **High.** Identifiable landmark |

### 3.3 What defines the style (my summary of the sources)

- **Stripped classicism (Wikipedia):** flat roofs, horizontal extension, uniformity and no décor, giving "simplicity, uniformity, monumentality, solidity and eternity." The article calls it "starved neo-Classicism" [F].
- **Massing and piers:** long horizontal blocks, rows of square piers, and repetition. The Zeppelin grandstand is the clearest example [F for piers]. Limestone is in the Tempelhof and Haus der Kunst sources [F].
- **Scale:** the Reich Chancellery Marble Gallery and the 1.2 km Tempelhof terminal [F].
- **Not sourced:** "ruin value" (Speer's idea that buildings should look good as ruins). The Wikipedia article only links to it [F, as a link only]. Treat it as unverified.

### 3.4 Less loaded analogues

| Building | Date | Closeness to the grammar | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Federal Triangle, Washington** (multiple federal buildings) | 1927–38 [F] | **Close.** Neoclassical; Indiana limestone; Tennessee marble columns; six-storey height rule; the Commerce building 305 m (1,000 ft) long in 1927 plan [F] | **Best substitute.** US federal, no Nazi history |
| Palais de Tokyo, Paris | 1937 Exposition [F] | **Weak.** The infobox gives Art Deco, not neoclassical or stripped classicism [F] | Only as a loose reference |
| EUR, Rome | Planned under Mussolini from 1936–37, works halted in 1942 [F] | **Close in grammar, but not neutral.** Palazzo della Civiltà Italiana ("Square Colosseum") 1938–43 [F] | **Avoid.** Fascist-era project; same loaded history as the German examples |

**Recommendation:** use Federal Triangle for the massing of a monumental stone block, and Prussian neoclassicism (section 6) for the colonnade. Neither needs the Nazi-era shapes.

### 4.3 Clash with the Venetian upper half

- **Gothic Revival and Venetian Gothic:** no clash. Ruskin's *Stones of Venice* made the Venetian style a Victorian model [S, venice-refs.md]. Keep the Victorian towers and the palazzi as one family.
- **Domes:** check against the upper half. If the upper half has domed Byzantine churches (St Mark's style), the cast-iron and stone domes sit beside them without clash. Pick one dome style for the lower loop, not three (est.).
- **Third Reich grammar:** a clash with the Venetian feel is possible; it is also the most sensitive choice. Avoid it (section 5).
- **Transition:** if the upper half and lower loop meet at the tunnel mouth, put a Gothic arch gate (Holstentor or Porta Nigra type) at the join. It reads as a border, not a clash (est.).

## 5. Hard limits for the game (legal and reputational)

### 5.1 Must avoid

- Swastikas and other Nazi symbols; Reich eagles; party, SS or other insignia; slogans; greeting forms.
- The Tempelhof eagle-shaped plan. The Haus der Kunst swastika-motif mosaic. The Zeppelin grandstand emblem. The military emblem on the Brandenburg Gate quadriga [F, Brandenburg Gate article: "fitted with a military-style emblem"].
- Recreations of identifiable Nazi landmarks: Zeppelin Field, Haus der Kunst, Reich Chancellery, Tempelhof, Olympiastadion. Use the grammar, not the building.
- Real institution or Olympic identity: the 1936 Olympic emblem, rings and signage; the Royal Albert Hall and St Pancras names on signage (avoid real names on buildings, per the brief).

### 5.2 German law (Strafgesetzbuch)

- **Section 86a(1) [F, gesetze-im-internet.de/stgb/__86a.html]:** "Punishable by up to three years' imprisonment or a fine" for anyone who uses symbols of unconstitutional organisations. **Section 86a(2)** says symbols include "flags, badges, uniform pieces, slogans and greeting forms" (German text).
- **Section 86(4) [F, __86.html]:** the exception for civic education, averting unconstitutional activity, art or science, research or teaching, and reporting on current events or history. **Note:** the exception is paragraph 4 of § 86 in the current text, not paragraph 3. The § 86a(3) cross-reference points to § 86(4) and (5).
- **Games [S, search summaries only]:** from 2018 the USK (German games rating body) reviews titles case by case for the exception, and the § 86a ban itself stayed in place. I did not open the USK or the Federal Ministry of Justice statement. Treat this as press-summary only.

**What this means for Ultrack (not legal advice):** the statute bans symbols, not architecture. A building with no insignia is not a § 86a question. The practical risk is in insignia, slogans and greetings, which must not appear in any texture, sound, UI or crowd. A recognisable Nazi landmark is a reputational call, not a statutory one, so the safe rule is to avoid it. If Ultrack is sold in Germany, get a German lawyer to check the final game against § 86a and the USK practice.
